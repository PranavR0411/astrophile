A search for radio transients in the VLA COSMOS field

Calibrating, imaging and mosaicing 7,040 VLA images of the COSMOS field to search for sources that vary in brightness.

One of my first major research projects was in radio astronomy, with Dr. Kunal Mooley during his time at Caltech. Until then I had worked mostly with optical and infrared data. Radio telescopes record visibilities, interferometric measurements that must be calibrated, cleaned and transformed before they resemble an image, while the atmosphere constantly perturbs the signal.

Learning the tools

I learned the Common Astronomy Software Applications (CASA) package to calibrate raw visibilities, flag bad data and reconstruct images by deconvolution, training on the radio afterglow of GW170817, the neutron-star merger seen in both gravitational waves and a relativistic jet. In parallel I learned the Astronomical Image Processing System (AIPS) and its Python interface, ParselTongue.

The transient search

The main project was a transient search in the COSMOS deep field using archival data from the Karl G. Jansky Very Large Array. Transients brighten or fade on short timescales: supernovae, tidal disruption events, fast radio bursts, AGN flares. COSMOS is well suited to finding them because its two-square-degree field was observed repeatedly between 2012 and 2014, giving 110 epochs across 64 pointings.

The CASA version required by the original pipeline was obsolete and unusable, and compatible AIPS/ParselTongue versions were also out of date, so I wrote a custom AIPS-based calibration and imaging pipeline. After imaging, I split each epoch into its 64 pointings and inspected all 7,040 images in CARTA, masking side lobes, residual calibration errors and other artefacts. A mosaicing script in AIPS, with careful control of flux normalisation, beam matching and overlap geometry, then stitched the pointings back into one COSMOS image per epoch.

A custom Python routine extracted around 10,000 sources per epoch. I built a cross-matching script to track sources across all epochs and matched them to the COSMOS optical/IR catalogue via SIMBAD, narrowing the list to about 20 candidates for closer inspection.

What we found

None of the candidate light curves showed a transient signature, which raised suspicion. Re-examining the calibration revealed the cause: the phase calibrator itself was variable during the observations, injecting artificial fluctuations into the data. We are now developing a strategy to correct for this variability and re-run the analysis; if successful, we plan to propose targeted VLA follow-up in the next observing cycle.
