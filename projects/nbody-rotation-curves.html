An N-body galaxy and the case for dark matter

Simulating a rotating galaxy from first principles to show that flat rotation curves need a dark matter halo.

Read the report (PDF)

For the final project of a computational methods course I designed my own astrophysics topic instead of choosing from the predefined list: a full N-body simulation of a rotating galaxy, showing that gravitational dynamics fail to match observed rotation curves unless a dark matter halo is included.

The model has a luminous exponential disk, a central bulge following a Hernquist profile, and a dark matter halo following an NFW profile.

In MATLAB I implemented pairwise gravitational forces, leapfrog integration of positions and velocities, energy and momentum conservation checks, and numerical stabilisation for large particle counts, measuring rotation curves by averaging azimuthal velocities in radial bins. Naive force calculation is expensive even for modest particle numbers, so I used vectorisation and cell-based interaction trimming to keep the simulation fast enough to visualise.

With only the disk and bulge, the rotation curve declines at large radii, as Newtonian gravity predicts for a mass distribution that falls off with radius. Adding an extended dark matter halo makes the outer galaxy rotate much faster, producing the flat rotation curve seen in real spirals.
