Unsupervised clustering of the Hipparcos H–R diagram

Recovering stellar populations from 118,218 Hipparcos stars with DBSCAN, validated against derived temperatures and radii.

Data

Methods
Exploratory statistics, feature selection, DBSCAN clustering

Read the report (PDF)

My first independent research came through a project-based elective on statistical data analysis at IIT Mandi. I chose an astronomical dataset, knowing my professor could offer limited support in that area.

I worked on the 118,218-star Hipparcos catalogue. From the full 2 GB ESA dataset I extracted astrometric and photometric parameters (RA, Dec, parallax, proper motions, Vmag, B–V, V–I) and computed distance from trigonometric parallax, absolute magnitude, luminosity and stellar mass using piecewise mass–luminosity relations. An exploratory analysis of all 77 parameters, including distributions and a full correlation heatmap, identified low-collinearity features for clustering. I then filtered unreliable entries (distance < 100 pc, proper motions above noise thresholds, B–V < 2).

On the H–R diagram (B–V against absolute magnitude) I applied DBSCAN with eps = 0.1 and min_samples = 100. DBSCAN needs no preset number of clusters and is robust to outliers, which suits unevenly sampled astrophysical data. It found three populations: an elongated main sequence holding about 99% of stars, a distinct red-giant locus, and a sparse group of cool, low-mass stars.

To validate this physically, I computed effective temperatures from the B–V colour–temperature relation and derived stellar radii. The red-giant cluster has mean temperatures around 4,400 K and large radii consistent with the giant branch, while the main-sequence cluster matches expected values for intermediate-mass stars.

This was my first end-to-end project with a major space-mission dataset, connecting raw astrometry to stellar evolution, and it taught me to find, read and implement methods from the literature largely on my own.
